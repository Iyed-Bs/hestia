# Design decisions

Hestia began as a university prototype: an ESP32 running a solar
electrolyser's thermal cycle, a dashboard, forecasting models and an
RL policy, built by a team of three. This log records what changed on the
way to a product, and why. Each entry: context, decision, consequence.

---

### ADR-001 · The controller decides safety; the gateway only grants a permit

**Context.** In the prototype, the controller obeyed plain-text commands
(`mode=MANUAL`, then `electrolyser=ON`) and manual mode drove the relays
directly, so whatever sent commands could run the electrolyser outside its
safe conditions. Energy decisions lived in the backend with no contract
limiting what it could ask.
**Decision.** Two layers. The controller (ESP32) applies every interlock
locally; the gateway's energy manager can only grant or withhold a
*production permit*, delivered as a lease that lapses after
`permit_valid_s` (120 s) unless refreshed.
**Consequence.** A crashed gateway, a lost network or a bug in the energy
logic can stop production, never make it unsafe.

### ADR-002 · One specification, two implementations

**Context.** The digital twin runs the controller in Python; the device runs
it in C++. Two copies of safety rules drift apart silently.
**Decision.** The rules live in `spec/controller_vectors.json` (19 scenarios,
step by step). Both implementations run it in CI. Command signatures get the
same treatment with `spec/command_vectors.json`, generated by the gateway's
own signer.
**Consequence.** "The twin behaves like the hardware" is a tested fact. A
change on one side fails CI until the other agrees.

### ADR-003 · The battery never drives electrolysis at night

**Context.** The prototype had a "thermal emergency → run the electrolyser on
battery" rule. Replaying a real January in the twin showed the battery
feeding the electrolyser through the night: 8.3 kWh drawn against 5.3 kWh of
solar that day.
**Decision.** The battery may only bridge a *running* electrolyser through
clouds, up to its minimum power; below 100 W of sun it is dusk, not a cloud,
and production stops.
**Consequence.** Battery → hydrogen → heat returns about 48 % of the energy;
the same electricity heats the building directly. Hydrogen is made from
solar surplus only, which is the point of storing it.

### ADR-004 · The isolation forest is not a leak detector

**Context.** The prototype shipped an isolation forest as `leak_detector.pkl`.
Testing the exported model showed that 300, 900 and 5 000 ppm of
H₂ get exactly the same score: trained on air that never exceeded ~55 ppm,
the forest saturates. The original scikit-learn model behaves the same, so
this is not an export artefact (`tests/test_ml_runtime.py::test_isolation_forest_cannot_see_a_leak`).
**Decision.** Leak detection is deterministic and lives on the controller:
two stages, each needing three consecutive readings (ADR-015). The model is kept,
renamed in the dashboard to what it can do: flag *unusual conditions*.
**Consequence.** No safety function depends on a statistical model. The
finding is documented rather than hidden.

### ADR-005 · Interlocks apply in manual mode; cooling stays on when too hot

**Context.** In the prototype, manual mode bypassed the interlocks, and the
over-temperature rule switched off *everything*, including the cooling pump,
the one thing that brings the temperature down.
**Decision.** Manual mode obeys the operator within the interlocks (no
electrolysis when too hot, electrolyte level low, hydrogen in the room,
storage at its limit or H₂ sensor unavailable). Over
temperature stops production and dosing and forces cooling *on*, in every mode.
**Consequence.** No command, from anyone, can produce an unsafe combination.

### ADR-006 · No production without a trustworthy hydrogen sensor

**Context.** Gas sensors drift, freeze, die and get poisoned, and a poisoned
sensor reads perfectly normal clean air. On small installations nobody
tracks bump tests or calibrations.
**Decision.** A trust layer judges the H₂ sensor continuously (frozen, dead,
drifting, implausible) and from its maintenance record (bump test every 30
days, calibration every 180, with a grace period). Pass or fail of a check
is computed from the measured numbers, not declared. An untrusted sensor
withdraws the permit at once.
**Consequence.** "Is the detector still working?" has a provable answer,
and the system acts on it. The twin can inject each failure, so the behaviour
can be demonstrated, and tested.

### ADR-007 · A tamper-evident journal, not a log file

**Context.** After an incident, an owner, inspector or insurer asks who reset
which alarm, when, and why, and whether the record has been edited.
**Decision.** Every safety-relevant event is appended to a journal where
each entry's SHA-256 covers the previous one, and an HMAC with a
gateway-only key covers each hash. Alarm resets require a written reason.
Reports print the chain head.
**Consequence.** Editing, deleting or reordering any past entry is detected
by `verify-journal`; a printed report pins the history as it was that day.

### ADR-008 · Signed commands on top of TLS

**Context.** The prototype used a public test broker on plain port 1883,
without logins: anyone who guessed the topic could send commands. TLS and
per-device logins fix that, but anyone with access to the broker (a
compromised device, a misconfigured ACL) could still publish commands.
**Decision.** Every command carries an HMAC-SHA256 over its sequence number,
timestamp and body. The controller refuses bad signatures, replayed sequence
numbers, timestamps outside ±30 s, and every command while its clock is
unsynchronised.
**Consequence.** Defence in depth: driving the electrolyser requires the
command key, not just network access. Without NTP the controller stays in
the safe state (no permit).

### ADR-009 · The twin replays a real measured year

**Context.** Synthetic sine-wave weather makes every demo look good and
proves nothing about clouds, heat waves or a cold week.
**Decision.** The twin and the planner use a real hourly year for Tunis
(Open-Meteo, 2025-05-24 → 2026-05-24), bundled with the code; the planner
and the simulator can fetch other locations. Durations use a forward-only clock, so jumping to
"winter night" never breaks a timer.
**Consequence.** The twin found real logic bugs (ADR-003) before any
hardware did.

### ADR-010 · ONNX at the edge, verified before loading

**Context.** The prototype loaded TensorFlow models and a pickle on the
gateway: heavy, slow to start, and a pickle can execute arbitrary code.
**Decision.** Models are exported to ONNX (checked against the originals) and
loaded only after their SHA-256 matches `manifest.json`. The runtime needs
onnxruntime and numpy, nothing else. Models load in the background; until
then, and if any fails, the energy manager uses its plain rules.
**Consequence.** Smaller image, faster start, no code execution from model
files, and ML that can only help.

### ADR-011 · The planner tells the truth

**Context.** The product is sold "for buildings": a buyer needs to know
whether hydrogen storage makes sense for theirs, and at what size.
**Decision.** The planner simulates a full year hour by hour for four
set-ups (today, solar, solar + battery, + hydrogen), sweeps electrolyser and
tank sizes, and states the result plainly, including when hydrogen does not
pay at today's prices. Every assumption is visible and editable.
**Consequence.** It sometimes argues against the sale. That is what makes
the rest of the numbers credible.

### ADR-012 · One process, one origin

**Context.** A building gateway is a small machine maintained by people who
are not system administrators.
**Decision.** The dashboard is built into the gateway and served from the
same origin as the API (strict CSP, no CORS); state is one SQLite file; the
deployment is one compose file with HTTPS (Caddy) and the broker (Mosquitto).
**Consequence.** Backups are one volume plus `.env`; updates are one
command; the attack surface is two ports.

---

The decisions below came from a second pass, when every number in the
twin and the planner was checked against a published model instead of being
taken on trust.

### ADR-013 · Physics from published models, shared by twin and planner

**Context.** The twin and the planner each had their own simplified formulas.
Checked against the bundled Tunis year, the twin's flat solar estimate
overstated production by about 18 %, and the planner's (0.80 × horizontal
irradiance, 1 439 kWh/kWp) understated it. The electrolyser was a fixed
yield per kWh, the tank had no pressure, the building one temperature.
**Decision.** One `physics/` package, each model with its source: sun
position (Spencer), Erbs diffuse fraction and HDKR transposition to tilted
panels, NOCT cell temperature and PVWatts losses; an alkaline stack with its
cell voltage, Faraday efficiency and heat (2.0 V per cell, 59.5 kWh/kg at
full load and 60 °C); KOH density and conductivity (Gilliam 2007); real-gas
tank pressure (Abel-Noble); a fuel cell, a hydrogen boiler, a heat pump
rated at the EN 14511 points; a two-node building (ISO 13790). The twin and
the planner both use it.
**Consequence.** 1 530 kWh/kWp a year at 30° in Tunis, inside the range
PVGIS gives. A stack has a minimum load (20 %), so the energy manager starts
it at 1.2 × that and stops it below, and the planner's yield changes with the
load. The planner and the simulator can no longer disagree about a building.

### ADR-014 · The electrolyte is KOH, measured by density

**Context.** The prototype controlled pH. A 30 % KOH solution sits above
pH 14, where glass probes saturate and read low. Conductivity was the
obvious replacement, but it peaks inside the operating band: at 25 °C, 26 %
and 28 % both read 0.626 S/cm, so the controller could not tell which way to
correct.
**Decision.** The controller keeps the KOH mass fraction between 25 and
32 %. Electrolysis consumes only water, so topping up with pure water
restores both the level and the strength; concentrate is dosed only while
the stack is stopped. The strength comes from a density transmitter
(4-20 mA, NAMUR fault limits), converted with the temperature by the same
formula on the ESP32 and in the gateway.
**Consequence.** A measurement that is monotonic over the whole band, and a
two-point calibration check in 20 and 30 % standards in the maintenance log.

### ADR-015 · Two-stage hydrogen detection with extraction

**Context.** The prototype had one alarm level at 500 ppm, which cut
everything, including any ventilation. Electrolyser rooms are normally
protected in layers: an early stage that ventilates, a later one that shuts
down.
**Decision.** Stage 1 at 500 ppm (1.25 % of the lower explosive limit)
stops production and starts an extraction fan, which runs until the air has
been clean for five minutes. Stage 2 at 2 000 ppm (5 % of the LEL) cuts the
power and latches, and the fan keeps running, because it is the only thing
that removes the gas. Both need three consecutive readings. The fan is
supplied from outside the electrolyser bus. A bump test must use gas above
the warning level and reach it to pass (ISEA practice).
**Consequence.** In the twin's 30 m³ room, a 2 L/min leak is held below the
alarm by the fan; a 20 L/min leak passes 2 000 ppm and latches; the same
small leak with a dead fan latches too. Each case is a test.

### ADR-016 · The cooling loop works while the stack produces

**Context.** The PFA bench produced until the electrolyte reached 60 °C,
then stopped and waited for 40 °C. On a 40 °C summer day in Tunis the
electrolyte can never get back to 40 °C, so the stack would never restart.
**Decision.** While producing, the cooling loop holds 50-55 °C (pump on at
55, off at 50). The stop-and-cool cycle stays as a fallback: at 60 °C the
stack stops, cools to 50 °C, has its electrolyte checked, and restarts. The
loop is sized with the stack (60 W/K per kW).
**Consequence.** The stack produces continuously at a steady temperature.
The old cycle now shows up only when something is wrong (a failed pump, a
loop too small for the weather), which makes it a useful alarm in itself.
The trust layer had to learn this too: a running stack that is being cooled
is not a stuck temperature probe.

### ADR-017 · Pressurised storage with an interlock and a relief valve

**Context.** "H₂ tank: 40 %" says nothing about safety. A tank is a pressure
vessel with a maximum allowable working pressure, and the gas in it warms up
in the sun.
**Decision.** The tank is defined by its volume and pressure rating; its
usable capacity is what reaches 95 % of the rating at 50 °C. Production
stops at 95 % of the rating, or if the pressure cannot be read; above the
rating a relief valve vents, and the venting is journalled.
**Consequence.** Sizes become physical: 4 kg at 30 bar needs about 1.9 m³,
20 kg almost 10 m³, and the planner says so next to its recommendation.

### ADR-018 · The live bench, and a simulator anyone can drive

**Context.** In the PFA, the demo could show heating, cooling, a winter
night or a leak. Turning the project into a product had narrowed the twin
down to one bench, and most of that was lost. A shared demo also cannot let
every visitor break things.
**Decision.** The live page shows the bench as it really runs. Separately,
each signed-in session, the read-only visitor included, can run a private
simulation of a whole building: its own engine, journal and trust layer, in
memory, with e-mail off. Thirteen scenarios start it in a situation worth
seeing; the visitor can also set up a site, change the time and speed,
inject faults and set conditions. A few simulations at most run at once, and
idle ones are closed.
**Consequence.** Every behaviour in this document can be shown on demand,
and nothing a visitor does reaches the live installation or its journal.

### ADR-019 · Heating and cooling: a fuel cell and a heat pump by default

**Context.** The prototype burned hydrogen for heat. With a heat pump in the
building, that is the weak use: a kWh of heat from the hydrogen boiler
replaces a heat pump that would have made it from a third of a kWh of
electricity.
**Decision.** By default the stored hydrogen feeds a fuel cell (45 %
electricity, 40 % heat recovered) that covers the building's shortfall,
heat pump included, after the battery. A hydrogen boiler is an option, and
it only takes over heat the heat pump could not make from the building's
own solar and battery. The heat pump heats and cools, with a COP that
follows the outdoor temperature. Comfort is 20-26 °C (EN 16798-1), and 5 %
of the hydrogen is kept for emergencies.
**Consequence.** For the demo building in Tunis the planner gives 96 %
self-sufficiency with the fuel cell against 93.5 % with the boiler, and says
why. Cooling, the larger need in a Tunisian summer, is part of every
calculation.

### ADR-020 · Where surplus sun goes: nature first, then cost

**Context.** Running the simulator on a summer day in Tunis, the battery
filled by 11:00 and the stack then took everything until the 4 kg tank was
full, two weeks into the season; after that, sun was simply curtailed. The
order battery → hydrogen → grid had been chosen by habit. Measured against
what each kWh actually achieves, it was wrong twice: a kWh sold to the
Tunisian grid displaces a gas-fired kWh (0.47 kg of CO₂), while the same
kWh through hydrogen comes back months later as 0.25 kWh of electricity and
0.22 kWh of heat, worth 0.15 kg. And the tank was too small to be seasonal.
**Decision.** Each destination is scored per kWh of surplus as *CO₂ avoided
× a carbon price + money saved or earned* (`domain/merit.py`). The carbon
price is 300 €/t, the climate cost the German Environment Agency recommends
(UBA 2024): high enough that nature decides whenever the CO₂ difference is
real, and cost when it is not. Surplus goes to the destinations in that
order; curtailment is last and the only true waste. In Tunis this gives
battery (0.43 kg, 0.081 €) → grid (0.47 kg, 0.035 €) → hydrogen (0.15 kg,
0.027 €); sales are capped at 30 % of production (Tunisian law 2015-12),
so hydrogen takes what the grid may not.
In Paris, where the grid is nearly carbon-free and power costs 0.25 €,
hydrogen comes before the grid. The battery leads in both: it loses 8 % by
tonight, hydrogen 75 % by winter. The planner and the simulator show the
table and say, at every step, where the power went and why. The demo
building moves to 8 kWp and a 10 kg tank, so hydrogen has a real season to
fill; it may be used when heating, when the tank passes 80 %, or in a grid
outage. Battery-first and hydrogen-first remain selectable, for comparison.
**Consequence.** Hydrogen is the seasonal store and the place for sun no one
else can take, which is how the one residential hydrogen system on the
market is built (HPS picea: a battery for the day, hydrogen for the
season). For the demo building, 17.8 kg a year raise self-sufficiency from
94 % to 99.7 % and avoid about 150 kg of CO₂ more; the planner still says
plainly that this costs more than it saves.
